Programmd programs prescribe effort as RIR. Most working sets sit at 2-3 RIR. The program prints the target and the lifter picks a load that meets it.

What RPE and RIR are, and how they differ

RPE stands for rating of perceived exertion. On the lifting version of the scale it runs from 6 to 10 and describes how close a set came to failure. RIR stands for reps in reserve: the number of reps still available when the set was stopped.

RPE and RIR measure the same thing from opposite ends. RPE counts effort up, RIR counts remaining reps down. Across this range, RIR is 10 minus the RPE. RPE 8 and 2 RIR describe the same set.

RIR is easier to answer in the moment because it asks for a count rather than a rating. RPE appears more often in printed programs and coaching notes, so most lifters end up reading both.

Reading the RPE scale in weightlifting

Judge the set on the last rep rather than on how the first few felt. Two signals carry most of the information: bar speed, and whether position held. A rep that slows sharply and starts to shift form puts the set at 9 or 10.

The rating is an estimate, and it is most accurate close to failure. At three or four reps in reserve lifters commonly over-rate the effort, so a set called RPE 7 often had a rep or two more in it than that.

How a reps in reserve chart gets used in a program

Programmd programs print the effort on every working set as an RIR number. You pick the weight that lands on it.

The same page still works when the load has to come down, or when it goes up. The chart is there to translate the number if a coach or a video gives you an RPE instead.
